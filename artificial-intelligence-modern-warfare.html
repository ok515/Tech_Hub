The Use of Artificial Intelligence in Modern Warfare: Transforming Conflict in the 21st Century

How autonomous drone swarms, neural ISR sensor fusion, decision-support algorithms, and cyber operations are reshaping battlefield dynamics and strategic stability.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping the nature of warfare at an unprecedented pace. From the battlefields of Ukraine to urban combat in Gaza and beyond, AI systems are no longer experimental concepts confined to laboratories or war games. They have become operational tools that enhance surveillance, accelerate decision-making, enable autonomous platforms, optimize logistics, and amplify both offensive and defensive capabilities. While AI promises greater precision, speed, and reduced risk to human soldiers, it also raises profound ethical, legal, and strategic challenges that could redefine the rules of conflict and the relationship between humans and machines in matters of life and death.

This article examines the primary applications of AI in contemporary military operations, real-world examples from recent conflicts, the strategic implications for future warfare, and the ethical and legal debates surrounding these technologies.

Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR)

One of the most mature and widely deployed uses of AI in warfare is in intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance. Modern conflicts generate enormous volumes of data from satellites, drones, ground sensors, social media, intercepted communications, and open-source intelligence. Human analysts alone cannot process this information at the speed required for timely decisions.

AI systems excel at fusing multi-source data, detecting patterns, identifying objects, and flagging anomalies. Computer vision algorithms can automatically classify vehicles, personnel, or infrastructure in imagery. Natural language processing tools analyze intercepted communications or social media in real time. Predictive analytics help forecast enemy movements or logistical vulnerabilities.

In Ukraine, AI-powered platforms have integrated satellite imagery, drone footage, and other sensor data to create dynamic operational pictures for commanders. Systems such as those developed with commercial partners have significantly shortened the sensor-to-shooter timeline. Similar capabilities have been employed by Israeli forces for target identification and tracking in complex urban environments.

"The integration of multi-source computer vision and real-time neural data fusion has compressed sensor-to-shooter loops from hours to under two minutes on modern battlefields."
— Defense Intelligence Review (2026)

These tools improve situational awareness and can reduce collateral damage when used carefully by enabling more accurate distinction between military and civilian objects. However, they also risk accelerating the tempo of operations beyond human cognitive capacity and introducing errors when training data is incomplete, biased, or deliberately manipulated by adversaries through deception or spoofing.

Autonomous and Semi-Autonomous Weapons Systems

Perhaps the most visible and controversial application of AI is in unmanned systems—drones, loitering munitions, ground robots, and maritime vessels. AI enables higher levels of autonomy in navigation, obstacle avoidance, target recognition, and even engagement decisions under certain conditions.

In the Russia-Ukraine war, both sides have fielded large numbers of first-person-view (FPV) drones and loitering munitions. Ukrainian forces have advanced AI-assisted navigation that allows drones to continue missions when GPS or radio links are jammed, using visual recognition for terminal guidance. Some systems can identify categories of military equipment and prosecute targets with reduced operator intervention. Russia has similarly pursued autonomous capabilities in its drone fleets. Drones now account for a substantial portion of casualties and strikes on both sides, demonstrating the shift toward attritable, mass-produced unmanned systems.

Israel has employed AI-enhanced drones for tracking and targeting, including systems that lock onto and pursue designated objects. Reports have described the use of AI in generating target lists and supporting rapid strike decisions. Other nations, including the United States, China, and various European countries, are investing heavily in collaborative autonomous systems and drone swarms that can coordinate without continuous human control of each platform.

Fully autonomous lethal weapons—systems that select and engage targets without further human intervention after activation—remain limited in scope and often restricted to specific environments (such as defensive anti-missile systems). Nevertheless, the trend toward greater autonomy is clear, driven by the need to operate in contested electromagnetic environments and to overwhelm defenses with mass.

Decision Support and Command and Control

AI decision-support systems (DSS) assist commanders by analyzing battlefield data, recommending courses of action, estimating collateral damage, and optimizing resource allocation. These tools can simulate thousands of scenarios rapidly, identify high-value targets, and support planning under time pressure.

In recent conflicts, AI has been used to generate targeting recommendations at scales previously impossible for human teams alone. Systems reportedly employed in Gaza have produced large volumes of potential targets based on surveillance data, with human operators reviewing recommendations. Similar tools support logistics, predictive maintenance of equipment, and training through realistic simulations.

RAND analyses highlight how AI could reshape core military competitions: favoring quantity (affordable mass of autonomous systems) over exquisite quality in some domains; intensifying the contest between hiding and finding through better sensor fusion and countermeasures such as AI-orchestrated deception; enabling more decentralized command through mission-type orders executed by autonomous agents; and altering the cyber offense-defense balance.

The U.S. military and NATO allies emphasize “human-in-the-loop” or “human-on-the-loop” principles for lethal decisions, while investing in AI to accelerate non-lethal processes. China and others are also pursuing AI-enabled command systems, including experimental virtual AI commanders in large-scale simulations.

Cyber Operations, Electronic Warfare, and Information Operations

AI strengthens both defensive and offensive cyber capabilities. Machine learning models detect anomalies in network traffic, predict vulnerabilities, and automate responses at machine speed. Offensively, AI can generate more sophisticated malware, scale phishing or social engineering, or optimize attacks on critical infrastructure.

In electronic warfare, AI enables adaptive jamming, cognitive radio techniques, and real-time spectrum management. In the information domain, generative AI produces deepfakes, synthetic media, and tailored propaganda at volume, complicating the information environment and potentially eroding trust.

These applications blur the lines between peacetime and wartime activities and raise risks of rapid escalation if automated systems respond to perceived threats without adequate human oversight.

Logistics, Training, and Force Multipliers

Beyond the tip of the spear, AI optimizes supply chains, predicts equipment failures, and improves resource allocation—critical for sustained operations. AI-driven simulations create adaptive training environments that prepare soldiers for complex scenarios more effectively than traditional methods. Demining systems using AI to analyze imagery accelerate clearance of contaminated areas, reducing risks to personnel.

Overall, AI acts as a force multiplier: it allows smaller forces to achieve effects previously requiring larger numbers of personnel, reduces exposure of human soldiers to danger, and compresses decision cycles.

Ethical, Legal, and Strategic Challenges

The integration of AI into warfare is not without serious concerns. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) and human rights organizations have highlighted risks associated with autonomous weapon systems (AWS), AI decision-support tools, and AI in cyber and information operations. Key issues include:

  • Loss of meaningful human control: When systems select and engage targets autonomously or with minimal oversight, accountability for violations of international humanitarian law (IHL) becomes difficult. Machines lack human judgment regarding context, proportionality, distinction between combatants and civilians, and the intrinsic value of human life.
  • Unpredictability and reliability: Machine learning systems can behave unexpectedly, especially in novel environments or under adversarial attack (data poisoning, spoofing). “Black box” opacity makes it hard to understand or explain decisions.
  • Bias and discrimination: Training data may embed biases that lead to discriminatory targeting patterns.
  • Acceleration of conflict and escalation risks: Faster decision cycles may leave less time for de-escalation or diplomatic intervention. Misperception or automated responses could trigger unintended escalation.
  • Proliferation: Lower costs and commercial dual-use technology make advanced capabilities accessible to a wider range of state and non-state actors.
  • Accountability and responsibility: Determining legal responsibility when an AI system contributes to unlawful harm remains unresolved under existing frameworks.

The ICRC has called for new legally binding rules to prohibit or strictly regulate certain autonomous weapons, particularly anti-personnel systems that target humans directly. Debates continue at the United Nations and among states, with positions ranging from calls for bans to emphasis on responsible use under existing IHL. Many military powers have issued ethical principles stressing human centricity, accountability, and reliability, yet practice often outpaces regulation.

Strategically, AI intensifies great-power competition. Nations that master AI-enabled mass, resilient networks, and adaptive command may gain decisive advantages. However, over-reliance on AI could create new vulnerabilities if systems are compromised or fail under pressure.

Looking Ahead

AI will not replace human judgment in war entirely in the near term, but it is already transforming how wars are fought. The conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East serve as live laboratories, demonstrating both the tactical benefits of AI-enhanced systems and the humanitarian costs when safeguards are insufficient. Future warfare is likely to feature greater reliance on attritable autonomous systems, AI-augmented decision-making at all levels, and intensified contests in the information and cyber domains.

The challenge for policymakers, military leaders, technologists, and the international community is to harness AI’s potential while preserving meaningful human control over the use of force, ensuring compliance with humanitarian law, and preventing uncontrolled escalation. Without clear norms, robust technical safeguards, and international cooperation, the promise of more precise and efficient warfare risks giving way to faster, more opaque, and potentially less accountable conflict.

The age of AI warfare has begun. How humanity governs it will shape not only the character of future battles but the broader relationship between technology and human agency in matters of security and survival.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q: How is AI currently being used in active conflict zones like Ukraine and Gaza?
A: In Ukraine and Gaza, AI is used for real-time sensor fusion, automated satellite/drone image recognition, terminal visual guidance for uncrewed platforms when GPS is jammed, and automated target prioritization and list generation for command teams.
Q: What is the primary risk of autonomous weapon systems (AWS)?
A: The main risks include the potential loss of meaningful human control, accountability challenges under International Humanitarian Law (IHL), unpredictable black-box behavior under adversarial electronic warfare, and potential escalation risks driven by compressed decision cycles.
Q: What does 'Human-in-the-Loop' mean in military AI decision-making?
A: 'Human-in-the-Loop' requires that a human operator makes the final lethal engagement decision, whereas 'Human-on-the-Loop' involves human oversight with the ability to abort an automated engagement, and 'Human-out-of-the-Loop' refers to fully autonomous execution without human intervention.
